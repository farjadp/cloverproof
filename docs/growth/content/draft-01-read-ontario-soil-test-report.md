# How to read an Ontario soil test report before you plan fertilizer

**Before you use a soil test report to plan fertilizer, check five things: which extraction method the lab used, what units the numbers are in, how deep the samples were taken, when they were taken, and whether the test is OMAFA-accredited. Ontario's fertilizer tables in Publication 811 only work with the accredited methods, so the numbers by themselves don't tell you enough.**

A report is a page of numbers, but those numbers depend on how they were produced. Two labs can test the same soil and report different phosphorus values because they used different chemistry. This guide covers what to look for on an Ontario report and why each item matters. It doesn't tell you what rate to apply. That comes from the official tables and your advisor.

## What is on a typical Ontario soil test report?

An Ontario field-crop report usually lists soil pH, buffer pH (used for lime decisions), phosphorus, potassium and magnesium, and often organic matter, zinc and manganese indices. Publication 811, Chapter 9, lists the OMAFA-accredited tests in Table 9-1, *OMAFRA-accredited soil tests*.

According to Field Crop News (OMAFA, 2025), eight tests are accredited:

| What is measured | Accredited method |
|---|---|
| Soil pH | Saturated paste or 1:1 water |
| Buffer pH (lime requirement) | SMP or Sikora |
| Phosphorus | Sodium bicarbonate (Olsen) |
| Potassium | Ammonium acetate |
| Magnesium | Ammonium acetate |
| Zinc index | DTPA, modified by soil pH |
| Manganese index | Phosphoric acid, modified by soil pH |
| Nitrate-N | Potassium chloride |

Source: [Field Crop News, "OMAFA-Accredited Soil Testing Program"](https://fieldcropnews.com/2025/05/omafa-accredited-soil-testing-program/).

## Which extraction method did the lab use?

Look for the method name next to each nutrient. For phosphorus in Ontario you want **Olsen** (also written "sodium bicarbonate" or "NaHCO3"). For potassium and magnesium, look for **ammonium acetate**.

This matters because an extractant is a chemical solution, and different solutions pull different amounts of a nutrient out of the same soil. Publication 811 explains that the Olsen solution has a pH of 8.5, while Bray-1, Bray-2 and Mehlich-3 solutions have a pH of 2.5. On alkaline soils the acidic extractants can overestimate available phosphorus. Pub 811 calls the Olsen method "more consistent in predicting P availability for the range of Ontario soils" ([Pub 811, Chapter 9](https://www.ontario.ca/files/2022-10/omafra-agronomy-guide-for-field-crops-chapter-9-en-2022-10-13.pdf)).

Pub 811 is direct about phosphorus: Bray or Mehlich-3 results "cannot be used" to determine recommendations from its fertility tables. Potassium is a little different. Pub 811 notes that Mehlich-3 extracts amounts of potassium comparable to the ammonium acetate method. Ask your advisor how they handle a Mehlich-3 potassium number before relying on it.

If the method isn't printed, ask the lab. Don't guess.

## What units are the numbers in?

Ontario reports give phosphorus, potassium and magnesium in **milligrams per litre of soil (mg/L)** or **parts per million (ppm)**. Pub 811 says the two are "approximately equal."

Watch for three things:

- **Soil test units are not fertilizer units.** A soil value of P in ppm is not a rate in pounds per acre or kilograms per hectare.
- **Elemental vs oxide form.** Soil tests report the element (P, K). Pub 811's cereal guidelines are written in the oxide form: Table 4-20 is *Phosphate (P2O5) guidelines for cereals* and Table 4-21 is *Potash (K2O) guidelines for cereals* ([Pub 811, Chapter 4](https://www.ontario.ca/files/2022-10/omafra-agronomy-guide-for-field-crops-chapter-4-en-2022-10-13.pdf)).
- **Labs from outside Ontario** may use different units or report on a different basis. Pub 811 notes that a non-accredited lab's results can be used with its tables only if the lab uses the identical test and expresses results in the same units. Even then, accuracy isn't assured.

## How deep and when was the soil sampled?

The report should state, or you should know, the **sampling depth** and the **sampling date**.

On depth, Publication 811 says samples should represent 15 cm (6 in.), with two exceptions: nitrate-nitrogen samples should represent 30 cm (12 in.), and for pH in continuous no-till fields with surface-applied lime, it describes a shallower 10 cm (4 in.) sample. A result from a different depth won't line up with tables built on the standard one.

On timing, Pub 811 says once every three years is adequate for most farms, often once per rotation at the same point in it. It also notes that sampling at the same time of year makes trends easier to track. Fields that lose a lot of potassium, or that recently had manure or a legume sod plowed down, may need more frequent or carefully timed sampling. Ask your advisor whether your test is still current for this crop.

Write the date down next to the field. A plan built on a six-month-old test and one built on a five-year-old test should not carry the same confidence.

## Is the test OMAFA-accredited?

Check whether the lab is on OMAFA's accredited list for soil testing. The program checks labs through external proficiency testing (run with the North American Proficiency Testing Program). Field Crop News states: "OMAFA-accredited soil tests will provide the most accurate fertilizer guidelines for Ontario field crops."

A non-accredited report isn't useless. You just can't feed it straight into Ontario's tables, and your advisor needs to know which method produced it.

## How does the report connect to a fertilizer decision?

A soil test answers "what does this soil supply?" The fertilizer decision also needs the crop, the yield goal, the field's history and the economics. For cereals, Pub 811 Chapter 4 reads phosphate and potash guidelines from accredited soil test values (Tables 4-20 and 4-21). Where manure has been applied, the fertilizer amount is reduced to account for it (see Chapter 9, Manure).

Nitrogen is different. A routine soil test doesn't give you a winter wheat N rate. Spring N for winter wheat is a separate decision built mainly on crop, field history and economics. See our guide to [planning spring nitrogen for Ontario winter wheat](/en/learn/spring-nitrogen-winter-wheat-ontario).

## What should you send your advisor?

Send the **whole report as a PDF or photo**, not a retyped table. Retyping drops the method names and units, which is how mistakes start. Add:

- the field name, and which report goes with which field
- sampling date and depth, if they aren't printed
- the lab name
- anything that changed since sampling (manure, lime, a plowed-down sod)

## FAQ

**Can I use a Mehlich-3 report with Ontario fertilizer tables?**
Not for phosphorus. Publication 811 says Bray or Mehlich-3 phosphorus results cannot be used with its fertility tables, which are built on the Olsen method. For potassium, Pub 811 notes that Mehlich-3 extracts amounts comparable to ammonium acetate. Ask your advisor how they treat it.

**How old can my soil test be?**
Publication 811 says sampling once every three years is adequate for most farms. Whether your test is still good for this field depends on what has happened since, such as manure, high-removal crops or lime. Ask your advisor, and record the sampling date with the plan.

**Is ppm the same as mg/L on a soil test?**
Publication 811 says soil test results in milligrams per litre (mg/L) and parts per million (ppm) are approximately equal. Neither is a fertilizer rate.

**Does a soil test tell me how much nitrogen to put on winter wheat?**
No. A standard report covers P, K, Mg, pH and some micronutrients. The spring N decision for winter wheat rests mostly on the crop, field history and economics. Pub 811 Chapter 4 covers cereal N.

**Where do I find accredited labs?**
OMAFA keeps the list of accredited labs on Ontario.ca. The Field Crop News article on the accredited program links to it.

**Can I plan fertilizer without a soil test at all?**
Nitrogen can be planned from field information, but with less certainty. Phosphorus and potassium decisions need a lab test. See [No soil test? What a plan can and cannot cover](/en/learn/fertilizer-plan-without-soil-test).
