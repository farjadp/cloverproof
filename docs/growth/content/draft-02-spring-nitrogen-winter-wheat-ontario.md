# Planning spring nitrogen for Ontario winter wheat: the decision, step by step

**Spring nitrogen for Ontario winter wheat comes down to a few steps: confirm the crop and stand, gather field history, set a realistic yield goal against fertilizer and grain prices, choose one pass or a split, adjust for your disease plan and lodging risk, and write down why. The numbers come from OMAFA Publication 811 and your advisor.**

This article walks through how the decision is made. It doesn't give rates. Ontario's guideline tables are in Publication 811, Chapter 4, and the right number for your field depends on inputs only you and your advisor have.

## What goes into a spring N decision?

Winter wheat is seeded in the fall, but its nitrogen decision belongs to spring. Publication 811 says fall nitrogen, beyond the small amount that comes with starter phosphorus, is "strongly discouraged": the trials it summarises in Table 4-16, *Fall-applied nitrogen*, showed no benefit, and fall N carries environmental risk ([Pub 811, Chapter 4](https://www.ontario.ca/files/2022-10/omafra-agronomy-guide-for-field-crops-chapter-4-en-2022-10-13.pdf)).

In spring, the decision draws on four groups of inputs:

| Input group | Examples | Where it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| Crop | Market class (soft red, soft white, hard red), seeding date, stand and tillering at green-up | Which Pub 811 table applies; early vs delayed first pass |
| Field history | Previous crop, manure, legume sod, last year's N | Adjustments to the N requirement |
| Economics and goal | Expected yield, nitrogen-to-wheat price ratio | How Pub 811 Table 4-14 is read |
| Risk | Disease control plan, lodging history, soil type | Rate and timing changes |

## Step 1: Confirm the crop and the stand

Start with the facts of the field this spring, not last fall's plan.

- **Market class.** Pub 811 Table 4-14, *Nitrogen requirements for pastry wheat (most profitable N application)*, covers soft red and soft white wheat. Hard wheat is grown for protein, and Pub 811 says it often needs a higher nitrogen rate to reach protein specifications. Use the right table for the class.
- **Seeding date and stand.** Field Crop News (OMAFA) notes that late-planted or poorly tillered fields may benefit from an early application at green-up, while early-planted, heavily tillered fields may benefit from a delay ([Follings, 2022](https://fieldcropnews.com/?p=24016)). Walk the field and assess the stand before you settle on timing.

## Step 2: Gather the field history

The previous crop and the field's recent past change how much N the wheat needs from fertilizer.

- **Previous crop.** Pub 811 Table 4-10, *Management considerations for wheat following various crops in rotation*, sets out what changes after each crop. After processing peas, for example, it notes residual nitrogen lowers the wheat's N requirement and that lodging may be an issue.
- **Legumes and manure.** Pub 811 Chapter 9 covers credits for legume sod (Table 9-9, *Adjustment of nitrogen requirement, where crops containing legumes are plowed down*) and for manure ([Pub 811, Chapter 9](https://www.ontario.ca/files/2022-10/omafra-agronomy-guide-for-field-crops-chapter-9-en-2022-10-13.pdf)).
- **Soil test.** A routine soil test doesn't set the wheat N rate, but you need it for the phosphorus, potassium and pH parts of the plan. See [how to read an Ontario soil test report](/en/learn/read-ontario-soil-test-report).

If you don't know part of the history, say so. A missing input should lower your confidence in the number. It shouldn't be quietly filled in.

## Step 3: Set the yield goal and the economics

Pub 811 Table 4-14 gives the most profitable N application from two inputs: **expected yield** and the **nitrogen-to-wheat price ratio** (the price of a unit of N divided by the price of a unit of wheat). So a sound rate depends on:

- a realistic expected yield for this field, based on its own history rather than the best year on the farm
- the N price you will actually pay and the wheat price you expect

When either price moves, the most profitable rate can move too. Write down the prices you used.

## Step 4: Decide one pass or a split

A single spring pass is simpler. Splitting means putting part of the N on early and the rest later. Field Crop News sets out the trade-offs ([Follings, 2022](https://fieldcropnews.com/?p=24016)):

- Depending on the year and field, a split may give a yield bump, or "little or no benefit."
- A single application can't be adjusted once yield potential becomes clearer, and a large rate in one pass raises the risk of N loss, lodging and disease.
- Early-applied N is more exposed to leaching and denitrification, with the greatest risk on sand and clay soils.
- The extra pass costs money, and any added yield has to pay for it.

Pub 811 adds that split applications can raise protein, but the gain is often too small to pay for the extra pass (see Table 4-15, *Managing for increased protein*). For timing, use growth stages rather than calendar dates. Grain Farmers of Ontario publishes a [winter wheat staging guide](https://gfo.ca/agronomy/crop-management).

## Step 5: Adjust for your disease plan and lodging risk

Pub 811 explains that more nitrogen makes a denser canopy, which raises the risk of foliar disease. It says yield responses to higher N rates aren't expected unless leaf and head diseases are controlled with fungicides, and that the spring rate can change where a fungicide is planned and lodging hasn't been a concern (see the note to Table 4-13, *Nitrogen requirements for cereal crops*).

So the N decision and the fungicide decision belong together. If you don't plan a fungicide, or the field lodges easily, factor that into the rate and timing.

## Step 6: Write down the decision and why

Record what you decided and the inputs you used, before the spreader goes out:

- the field, the crop and the class
- the inputs: stand notes, previous crop, manure, soil test (method, depth, date), yield goal, prices
- the table or method behind the number, and its edition
- the rate, product, timing and split
- who made or approved the decision
- any later change and the reason for it

A record like this lets you compare planned with applied, explain the decision later, and learn from the season. Our guide to [keeping proof of fertilizer decisions](/en/learn/fertilizer-record-keeping-proof) goes further.

## FAQ

**Can I put nitrogen on winter wheat in the fall?**
Publication 811 strongly discourages fall nitrogen on Ontario winter wheat, apart from the small amount that comes with starter phosphorus. The trials it summarises (Table 4-16) showed no benefit.

**Does the previous crop change the wheat N decision?**
Yes. Pub 811 Table 4-10 lists what changes after common Ontario crops, and Chapter 9 covers adjustments for legume sod and manure. Ask your advisor how to apply them to your field.

**Should I split spring nitrogen on winter wheat?**
It depends on the field and the year. Field Crop News (OMAFA) describes when an early first pass or a delay makes sense, and notes that a split adds a pass that has to pay for itself.

**Does my fungicide plan change the N decision?**
It can. Pub 811 says higher N rates aren't expected to pay unless leaf and head diseases are controlled, and that the spring rate can change where a fungicide is planned and lodging isn't a concern.

**Where do I find Ontario's N tables for wheat?**
In OMAFA Publication 811, Chapter 4, Cereals: Table 4-13 (cereal crops) and Table 4-14 (pastry wheat).

**Does hard red winter wheat need a different plan?**
Pub 811 says hard wheat often needs a higher nitrogen rate to meet protein specifications. Plan it with your advisor and your buyer's protein target in mind.
